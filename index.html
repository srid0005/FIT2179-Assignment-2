Malaysia's Flood Crisis

Monsoons are predictable. The scale of devastation is not.

Every year, monsoon rains push Malaysia's rivers over their banks. Most years, a few thousand people are displaced. Some years, hundreds of thousands. And in 2021, a single week of rain in the Klang Valley caused RM 6.1 billion in losses — 0.40% of national GDP. This is the story of 60 years of flooding, in four acts.

1. The Geography 2. The Pattern 3. The Human Cost 4. A Deepening Crisis

Act 1

Some states bear far more than their share

Floods are not evenly spread across Malaysia. Four states — Johor, Pahang, Terengganu, and Kelantan — together account for nearly three out of every four recorded flood events since 1965. The pattern is dictated by geography: these are the states most exposed to the Northeast Monsoon and crossed by the country's largest river basins. Hover any state for the full count.

Darker bars mark the four hardest-hit states (18+ events).

Notice: Despite their size, Sabah and Sarawak record fewer events in EM-DAT than the small east-coast peninsular states. River basin geography — not land area — drives flood exposure.

Act 2

Floods follow the monsoon calendar

Malaysia's floods are seasonal, not random. The Northeast Monsoon (November–March) lashes the east coast and Borneo with prolonged rain. The Southwest Monsoon (May–September) is gentler but still produces flash flooding. December is the single most dangerous month: 21 of 74 recorded floods began in December alone, and they were also the most severe.

Each row is a state; each cell is a calendar month. Darker = more flood events. Filter by season using the dropdown above the chart.

Bubble size = number of events. Y-axis = average people affected per event (log scale).

Notice: December has both the most events and the most damaging events — a single December flood affects an average of 62,361 people, more than ten times the annual mean of other months.

Act 3

More events, more people, more often

Since 2001, Malaysia has seen flooding accelerate sharply. Before 2001, only one or two events per year were significant enough to make EM-DAT's threshold. Since 2016, that figure is consistently four to eight events per year. The 2014 Kelantan floods and the 2021 Klang Valley floods stand out as the two worst single-year displacement events in modern Malaysian history.

Bars (left axis) = events per year. Red line (right axis) = total people affected per year.

Hover any bar for full location details. Drag the slider above the chart to see more or fewer events.

Click any flood-type in the legend to focus on it.

Broad-scale monsoon floods dominate; flash floods are rarer but proportionally far more dangerous in urban areas.

Notice: The five worst events of the past 60 years all happened in November or December — and four of them since 2014. The Northeast Monsoon is not getting wetter on average, but its extreme tails are growing.

Act 4

Economic losses are shifting and growing

Beyond the human toll, floods now cost Malaysia hundreds of millions of ringgit every year. The 2021 Klang Valley event was an order of magnitude worse than any other — RM 6.1 billion, or 0.40% of GDP. But 2024 tells a quieter, more troubling story: total losses rose to RM 933 million, with the burden shifting decisively from Selangor and Pahang to Kelantan, Kedah, and Terengganu.

Log scale: the 2021 bar would be 10× the height of others on a linear axis. Hover for category breakdowns.

Kelantan alone took 28% of the 2024 national loss.

Red lines slope up = state got worse 2022→2024.

Notice: 12 of 16 states recorded higher losses in 2024 than 2022. Kedah's tripled. Pahang and Terengganu fell, but only because their 2022 figures were exceptionally high — both remain well above the national median.

The pattern is no longer a surprise

The monsoon comes every year. The rivers overflow every year. What changes is how prepared the country is — and which communities bear the worst of it. The data here covers 60 years; the next 60 will be shaped by how seriously we read it.
